## Pakistan saves. It just doesn't invest.

The licence was real. The funds were already trading. What was missing was everything between a licence and a customer.

Because this was never an investing problem. It was a trust problem. People in Pakistan already save for the future: gold kept at home, money left sitting in bank accounts, long-term plans built around what they know. Moving that into an app meant trusting products, financial language and decisions they were not used to making alone, with no branch to walk into. So the work was to create an experience seamless enough that nobody would need one.

## The Design System

The design system was what made Mahaana work at scale. Four platforms, the products that had already shipped, and a roadmap of ones that had not, all pulling on the same interface. That needed one set of foundations, components and page patterns rather than a fresh interpretation per product. The system was built for dark mode as well as light, with every colour defined as a token that has a value in both themes, so nothing had to be redrawn for the second one. Held in one place, a change lands on every product, platform, breakpoint and theme at once, at the same level of quality.

## Ten minutes, and nothing skipped

Before redesigning anything, I ran usability tests on the staging app and mapped how each stage of onboarding felt, from the splash screens to the last step.

<MahaanaJourneyMap />

Onboarding averaged a UX score of 78. The look and most screens were fine; the complaint was length, and bugs made it worse. The regulated steps were not ours to remove, so the question became how to make every required step feel worth finishing.

<MahaanaOnboarding />

The redesign answers that in two ways. Some steps explain before they ask: the first screen offers Save+ against a bank account and Retirement against the income tax it saves, and set-up names its two parts before the first question. Others ask for less: risk questions come one to a screen, the ID card is photographed instead of typed out, and the answers turn straight into an allocation someone can see and change.

And the length had a target: ten minutes to invested, not to submitted, so the first investment is chosen inside onboarding, before the review. An account that opens and never gets funded is still a drop-off, just harder to see.

## Everything they own, on one screen

Once the money was in, the question people brought back to the app changed. It was no longer how to start. It was how it was going.

So the home screen is built as the questions someone brings to it, in the order they ask them, top to bottom.

How much do I have? The total value comes first, with the return beside it in rupees and as a percentage, over a chart that reads from a month back to all time. Where is my money? Each product follows on its own card with its own value, return and risk level, so someone holding only Save+ and someone holding all four read their home the same way.

Below the cards the screen shifts from checking to understanding. The day's market data (KSE100, the dollar, oil and gold) is there for the reason behind a number. For anyone unsure what to do next, Wealth Advisor books a call with a person, the nearest thing to a branch in an app that has none. And for people new to investing, the videos, explainers and news from Mahaana's desk sit last, out of the way for anyone who only came to check.

## Holding it together

Products that ship years apart only feel like one product if the pieces are shared. The system stays deliberately small: three brand colours and two type roles, composed into the cards, list rows and account picker that every journey reuses.

<MahaanaScreenWall />

Gold and trading were still ahead of us when this was built, which is the part I would argue for hardest: the pattern for a product tile existed before there was a gold product to put in one. New products arrive into a shape that is already decided, instead of arguing for their own.

Building the libraries this way cut design-to-development handoff by 35%, mostly by ending the round-trip where engineering has to ask what a spacing value or a state is supposed to be.

## What I took from it

The design system is what let the translation survive four platforms. Simplifying a pension or a cash fund is easy to do once, in one screen, in a design file. Doing it as components that hold their meaning in every product they appear in, built by someone else, months later, is the part that took the work.

What I would do differently is start the compliance conversation earlier. We treated the Shariah ruling as something to communicate well once the product existed. It would have been better as an input to what the product was, from the first sketch.

These numbers are a snapshot, and numbers age: a rating moves, a competitor ships, an interface starts to look like its year. What I would want read from them is the how, not the score. Trust was treated as something to design for rather than something to claim, and every claim on the screen had to be one the licence, the custodian and the compliance ruling could actually back.
