## Making one icon move is easy. Making a whole set feel consistent takes work.

An animation can look good on its own and feel out of place in an interface. Different timings make icons feel unrelated. Too much movement makes them distracting. Details that read clearly in a large preview can disappear inside a small button.

I wanted each icon to move in a way that suited its meaning, while keeping the collection consistent. Developers also needed a simple way to preview an animation, add it to a project and adapt it.

---

<TocGroup label="Solution" />

## Start with the object

I used Phosphor’s existing glyphs as the foundation. My contribution was the motion and its implementation: deciding which parts should move, where they should pivot and how they should settle.

A bell swings from its crown. An anchor sways from its ring. Each animation follows the shape and behaviour of the object. Shared timing and easing rules help those different movements belong together.

<IconimateHoverGrid />

## Protect the resting state

Every icon had to match its original Phosphor glyph at rest. This let it sit alongside static icons without changing the visual style of an interface.

That rule also helped me choose between ideas. For the beer stein, I explored a lid-flip animation but shipped a version that moved the whole mark, preserving the original path. The more elaborate version stayed in the experiments.

<IconimateRestStateGrid />

## Build enough variations to make a considered choice

For the baby carriage, I built seven candidates, exploring a simple pop, rolling, rocking and canopy movement.

The final version used a suspension bounce: the body dropped, the canopy followed and the tyres stayed planted. It gave the movement a clear physical relationship between the parts.

<IconimateLabCandidates />

Comparing the candidates side by side helped me judge where the motion started, how much movement was enough and how the icon returned to rest.

## Test at the size that matters

I compared animations at 16, 20, 24, 32 and 48 pixels, using 24px as the main reference. If a movement only worked in the larger preview, it needed another pass.

<IconimateSizeScale />

At that size, small decisions become obvious. A rotation can obscure the shape. A delay can make the movement feel disconnected. I adjusted the animations around those details so the icons remained recognisable throughout.

## Make the work usable

I built a searchable gallery where people can preview the animations before choosing an icon. Individual components can be installed through the shadcn CLI, giving developers the source code to adapt within their own projects.

<IconimateCardDemo />

The components also expose animation controls, allowing motion to respond to a parent button, card or touch interaction. That makes the animation useful beyond the gallery’s hover previews.

<TocGroup label="Result" />

## Put it out into the world

<IconimateImpact />

The collection has grown to 216 icons and received 45 GitHub stars. It was also featured on DEV.to’s social media, giving the project recognition beyond my own channels.

These are early signs of interest. Understanding how people use the icons in their products is the next step.

## Reflection

Iconimate became a practical way to improve at design engineering. I had to take responsibility for the whole experience: how an icon moved, how the component behaved and how someone would find and install it.

The strongest decisions came from comparing alternatives. Building seven versions of an icon gave me a clearer reason for choosing one. Constraints such as preserving the original glyph and checking movement at 24px helped me decide when an animation was ready.

Next, I want to gather feedback from developers using Iconimate in real interfaces. That will help me decide which icons to add, which behaviours to refine and where the installation experience needs work.
